# The method

Why this works, and how to get the most out of it.

## The core idea

You cannot write your taste into a prompt. People try — "write in a punchy,
authentic voice" — and get generic output, because those words mean nothing
specific. Taste is thousands of small preferences you have never articulated
and mostly cannot.

But you have already demonstrated it. Every reel you saved is a data point. The
saved folder is a record of your judgment, collected honestly over months,
without you performing for anyone.

The method is: take that record, analyze it as *video*, and extract the
transferable structure. Then keep it separate from a second file describing what
you can actually prove — so the agent knows both what good looks like and what
you are allowed to say.

## Choosing your reels

This is the highest-leverage decision in the entire kit. Everything downstream
inherits it.

**Pick reels you admired, not reels that performed.** Different things. A reel
can hit ten million views on a format you would be embarrassed to make. If you
train on borrowed taste you get borrowed scripts, and you will not enjoy making
them for long.

**Use two or three creators, not one.** This is not optional, and it is the part
people skip.

With one creator you cannot tell which parts are *structure* and which are
*personality*. Everything looks essential. Put a second creator beside them and
the picture separates immediately: the things both do are grammar, and the
things only one does are style. A third confirms it.

One creator gives you an impression. Three give you a grammar.

**Pick creators who differ.** The most useful pairing is two people who solve
the same problem in opposite ways — a fast, evidence-dense 30-second format
against a slow, framework-driven 100-second one. Where they disagree, you are
forced to choose, and that choice is your actual position.

**8–12 reels each, 15–25 total.** Fewer than ten and the pacing numbers are
noise rather than pattern. Past thirty you are paying for redundancy.

## What the analysis extracts

**Measured facts** — duration, hard cuts, average composition length, words per
minute. Real numbers from real files, per creator, never averaged across them.

**Shared grammar** — what every video does regardless of who made it. The
closest thing to laws.

**Differences** — where the creators diverge, and which side you take. A row
where you take neither side is a row you have not thought about yet.

**Engines** — each creator's repeatable beat sequence, its strength, and the
risk of copying it wholesale.

**Visual modes** — the small closed set of presentation modes actually in use,
usually four to six. The realization that surprises people: these videos feel
varied because the modes are *sequenced* differently, not because every sentence
gets a new design.

**Synthesis** — what you take from each, what you explicitly leave behind, and
your own default beat formula.

## The risk field

Every engine entry carries `risk_if_copied_wholesale`, and it is the most
valuable sentence in the document.

Every strong creator's format has a failure mode when worn by someone who has
not earned it. The fast tool-demo format, copied straight, makes you a faceless
aggregator with no worldview — replaceable by the next account with the same
tips. The framework-teacher format, adopted before you have results, reads as a
guru performance, and audiences detect that faster than you expect.

Knowing the format is not enough. You have to know what it costs.

## Steal the grammar, never the skin

The single rule underneath all of it.

**Grammar transfers:** beat sequence, pacing, where proof sits relative to the
claim, when to cut back to your face, how many ideas one composition carries.

**Skin does not:** phrasing, catchphrases, color palette, personality, specific
topics.

The test, applied to every lesson in your corpus: *can I state this without
naming that creator's specific topic?* If not, you have written down the skin.
Rewrite it until it transfers.

## Then argue with it

The analysis is a draft, not a verdict. Open `corpus/corpus.md` and edit it.

Where you disagree with the machine, you are right and it is wrong — it measured
what it could see, and you know why you saved those reels. Writing your
disagreement in is the step that turns a competent analysis into *your* corpus.

## Why voice.json is separate

The corpus is about other people. `voice.json` is about you.

Merging them seems tidy and is a trap: an analysis of creators you admire
quietly becomes a set of claims about yourself, and you end up with a script
agent confidently describing achievements that belong to someone else's career.

The separation also creates the guardrail. `proof[]` is a closed list. The agent
may only assert what traces back into it. Everything else gets flagged rather
than invented — which is exactly the behavior you want from something writing
claims you will say out loud, on camera, with your name on it.
